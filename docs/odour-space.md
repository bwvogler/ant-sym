# Odour space: colony odour is a blend with a few dimensions that matter, and aggression teaches the perceiver, not the colony's smell

*Research note for epic `ja-zg1u` (PRD §91; the owner's direction on `ja-s37s`, 2026-11-25). Written 2026-11-25, against code at `0597d51b`. Read in full text: Esponda & Gordon 2015, Bos & d'Ettorre 2012, Martin et al. 2008 (abstract and methods), Martin et al. 2011, Tsutsui et al. 2000 and 2003, Giraud et al. 2002, Frizzi et al. 2015, van Wilgenburg et al. 2010 (the parts quoted). Abstract only, so every number is **unverified**: Newey et al. 2010, Gordon 1989, Bey et al. 2025, Silverman & Liang 2001, Buczkowski et al. 2005, Thomas et al. 2006, Pedersen et al. 2006, Drescher et al. 2010, Fürst et al. 2012, Neupert et al. 2018, Leonhardt et al. 2007, Guerrieri et al. 2009, Eyer et al. 2021, Roux et al. 2013. Not reached: Langen et al. 2000 (Springer, no abstract in any open index), Reeve 1989, Martin & Drijfhout 2009, Sturgis & Gordon 2012 (titles checked through Crossref only). The owner's rule applies: where a source goes against the idea, it is written down.*

The owner asked (2026-11-25) to "widen odour space and drive scents away when there is aggressive contact, or some other mechanic grounded in literature; more dimensions makes sense". Today a colony's odour is one number in [0, 1] and two ants are strangers when their odours are further apart than 0.35 (`TOLERANCE`, `recognition.ts`). Measured by a 200,000-draw Monte Carlo over uniform odours (scratch script, not in the repo): two random colonies are kin with probability **0.578**, a wild colony (hash odour) is a stranger to both start colonies (at 0 and 1) only **30%** of the time, and the largest set of mutual strangers is about **3** colonies (0, 0.5, 1). The question here: *how many things does a real colony's odour have, how do real ants judge a difference, and does contact with a rival change either?*

## 1. A colony's odour is a blend of cuticular hydrocarbons, a point in a many-dimensional space

- Recognition cues are cuticular hydrocarbons (CHCs): alkanes, methyl-branched alkanes and alkenes. Bos & d'Ettorre 2012 describe the label as "multicomponent". Esponda & Gordon 2015 give the geometry the literature uses: "CHC-space, an n-dimensional Euclidean vector space in which each coordinate axis represents the concentration of a given hydrocarbon ... a particular chemical profile is a point in this multi-dimensional space", and the template-similarity model "describes recognition as a function of the distance between an individual's internal template ... and the cues that individual detects".
- **Against "many equal dimensions":** the space is large but few directions in it do the work.
  - *Formica exsecta*: ten colonies whose profiles are "almost entirely a Z9-alkene and alkane component"; aggression was elicited only by the alkene part, and changing the abundance of a single Z9-alkene on a live ant raised the aggression it got from nestmates in all five colonies tested. The authors conclude recognition "has evolved to rely upon highly sensitive responses to relatively few compounds" (Martin et al. 2008, Proc R Soc B).
  - Argentine ants: homologous compounds (same methyl position, different chain length) occur in correlated concentrations and "convey the same information" (van Wilgenburg et al. 2010, J Chem Ecol).
  - *Brachymyrmex patagonicus*: aggression between colonies tracked their genetic difference but not their overall chemical differentiation, "which may suggest that only a few chemical compounds influence nestmate recognition" (Eyer et al. 2021, Ecol Evol; abstract, **unverified**).
- No paper in this pass gives a number for the effective dimensionality of a colony's odour. "A few" is the reading of these three.

**In the game:** the odour becomes a short vector, **K = 3** to start (the number is **design**; §6 shows why), one axis for a *class* of compounds (say the dominant alkene, the methyl-branched series, the diet-borne part), not for single hydrocarbons. Each axis has a **weight** (default 1), a per-species trait, so a species can recognise on one axis only (Martin 2008) without the code changing. K = 1 with weight 1 is today's game, bit for bit (the "off" setting). The sense is the existing one (vision.md, "Cuticular hydrocarbons (contact)"): no new sense row.

## 2. Similarity is a template-to-label distance against a threshold, not an identity match

- Ants compare the label on another's cuticle with a neural template of their own colony's; "typically, ant workers attack the encountered individuals when the perceived differential exceeds a certain threshold; below this threshold, the encountered individual is accepted or simply ignored" (Bos & d'Ettorre 2012, citing Sherman et al. 1997 and van Zweden & d'Ettorre 2010). Reeve 1989's acceptance-threshold model is the origin of this reading (title checked in Crossref, not read).
- The colony template is not fixed: it is held in the central nervous system and adjusts slowly (more than 2 h after a workers' antennae were loaded with a foreign gland extract, 15 h to accept it) without losing the old label as nestmate (Leonhardt et al. 2007, J Comp Physiol A; abstract, **unverified**).
- **The distance is not obviously symmetric.**
  - For: *Camponotus herculeanus* rejected nestmates bearing an *added* dimethyl alkane and did not reject ants lacking one; "they do not specifically recognize nest-mates, but rather recognize and reject non-nest-mates bearing odour cues that are novel to their own colony" (Guerrieri et al. 2009, Proc R Soc B; abstract, **unverified**).
  - Against: in *C. floridanus*, sub-colonies given one or two extra compounds learned them as nestmate labels, and a worker from the two-compound sub-colony attacked ants with only one, so "additional components as well as the lack of a component can alter a label as non-nestmate" (Neupert et al. 2018, Front Behav Neurosci; abstract, **unverified**). That paper also finds ants hold **several** templates and accept both the old and the new label.

**In the game:** a contact is read as a **distance in the K-space** from the perceiver's own odour (its template: today's code already compares the other ant's band centre with the perceiver's own odour, `battle.ts`) to the other's, `d = sqrt(sum_k w_k (o_k - t_k)^2)`, and the other is a stranger when `d > tolerance + STRANGER_EPS`. The tolerance stays 0.35 and stays per ant (the fungus's B4 lever widens it). A **one-sided** distance (only the perceiver's missing dimensions count) is possible and would give directed aggression, which `contact-actions.md` already models; the sources split on it, so it is left to an owner decision (§ Open questions). Default: symmetric.

## 3. Recognition is distributed: each ant has its own boundary, and experience moves it

- Esponda & Gordon 2015 (full text) model recognition like the vertebrate immune system: "no single ant knows the odour identity of the colony ... Each ant responds to the odour of other ants by reference to its own unique decision boundary, which is a result of its experience of encounters with other ants". In the model, "ants habituate to an odour if each encounter with the odour is not associated with aggression, or if there is a positive experience such as trophallaxis or grooming, while aggression or the absence of a positive experience leads to gradual sensitization to non-nestmate odours." It is a model: its predictions (variable assays, dependence on the number of ants tested, change over time and with experience) are what the authors say the earlier experiments show.
- Counter-case from the same paper: *Cataglyphis niger* reduced its aggression to a particular non-nestmate over successive encounters (Nowbahari, cited there; not read).
- Task matters: exterior workers are more aggressive than interior ones, and recognition is more likely towards neighbours' ants (Sturgis & Gordon 2013, as Esponda & Gordon cite it; not read).

**In the game:** the game already has a per-ant tolerance array (`Colony.tolerance[i]`), set from the colony's and moved by the fungus. Habituation and sensitisation are a **per-ant, perceiver-side** change to that boundary, the cue is "a contact that ended in a fight" (sensitises) or "in calm" (habituates), both local. This is the home of the owner's "aggressive contact changes things" (§5), not the colony's smell.

## 4. Neighbours: "nasty neighbour" is common, "dear enemy" is not what most ants show, and neither is an odour shift

- ***Pogonomyrmex barbatus***: "encounters on foraging trails with ants from neighboring colonies deter foraging more than encounters with ants from distant ones" (Gordon 1989, Oecologia; abstract, **unverified**): neighbours are distinguished from strangers.
- ***Oecophylla smaragdina***: colonies recognised a greater share of neighbours' workers as non-members, and showed more aggression to them once recognised, "evidence that differential levels of aggression involve both a perceptual and a behavioral component". No rapid learning of new colonies in 10 days of contact. "The nasty neighbor effect may be much more common than the dear enemy effect among group-living species" (Newey et al. 2010, Ecology; abstract, **unverified**).
- ***Crematogaster scutellaris***: read in full. 34 nests fell into 14 genetic colonies; CHC profiles differed between colonies; "aggressiveness increased with both spatial proximity and similarity in cuticular hydrocarbon profiles among colonies, suggesting a nasty neighbor effect"; chemical distance was not correlated with spatial distance (Mantel r = -0.049, P = 0.73) nor with genetic distance, and the authors read CHCs as "linked to external environmental factors rather than genetic relationships" (Frizzi et al. 2015, PLoS One). **This goes against the idea that neighbours' odours move apart**: the neighbours that fought most were the chemically *closest*.
- **Against "nasty neighbour" as the rule:** *Myrmica rubra* aggression "increases with distance (geographical, genetic and chemical)" (Fürst et al. 2012, Proc R Soc B; abstract, **unverified**), a dear-enemy-like pattern; the fire ant *Solenopsis saevissima* neighbours showed both effects (Roux et al. 2013, Behav Processes; abstract); two *Pheidole* species showed habituation and dear enemy (Langen et al. 2000, cited by Esponda & Gordon; **not read**).

**In the game:** who is a neighbour is already local (a contact at the border). The neighbour effect is a **perceiver** effect (§5), and the game may show nasty neighbours (the commoner finding) without any change to colony odour. Dear-enemy is the habituation half of §3 and is what the fungus's calming levers already buy.

## 5. Aggression teaches the perceiver an enemy template; the sources do not show it moving the colony's odour

- Bey et al. 2025 (Curr Biol; abstract only, **unverified**): "associative learning plays a crucial role in the formation of both nestmate and non-nestmate recognition templates and ... the aggression received by an ant acts as an unconditioned stimulus that the ant likely associates with the odor label of its enemy". They propose this explains the nasty-neighbour effect.
- Neupert et al. 2018 (abstract): ants learn several labels as nestmate; Leonhardt et al. 2007: templates adjust in hours.
- **Searched for, not found:** a measurement that a colony's CHC profile shifts *because* it was in fights, or that two neighbouring colonies' profiles move away from each other with contact. Europe PMC and PubMed searches ("ant cuticular hydrocarbons change after fighting", "character displacement cuticular hydrocarbons ants", "divergence between neighbouring colonies") returned nothing on point. The only chemical-divergence mechanisms found are in §6.

**Verdict on the owner's first idea ("drive scents away on aggressive contact")**: **not supported.** What the sources do support for the same visible outcome (a war that leaves the two sides *less* able to make peace) is a change in the **perceiver**: an enemy template learned from aggression and a boundary that sensitises (§3, Bey 2025, Esponda & Gordon 2015). The evidence that neighbours' *scents* diverge by contact is absent, and Frizzi et al. 2015 (closest colonies fight most) and the diet work in §6 (shared environment converges scents) lean the other way.

**In the game:** a **learned foe**, per ant: when an ant is attacked by (or fights) a stranger, it stores that stranger's odour vector as a foe template, with a strength that decays over minutes; strangers close to the foe template then count with extra weight (and the borderline "near kin" lose their benefit of the doubt). It is perceiver state, local, and needs no global read. All numbers are **design**; the sign and the mechanism are from Bey 2025 and Esponda & Gordon 2015, and the counter-evidence (no rapid learning in weaver ants: Newey 2010; Argentine-ant fights dissolving after a shared diet: §6) is why it is **staged last and behind a setting** (§ The mechanic, S4).

## 6. What does move a colony's odour: separation, diet and the shared environment, over days to weeks

- **Isolation plus different food diverges odours.** Argentine-ant colony fragments, isolated and fed prey with different CHCs, attacked their former nestmates; isolation of 28 days or more kept them apart for at least 28 days more after a bridge, while fragments isolated less than 28 days reunited (Silverman & Liang 2001, Naturwissenschaften; abstract, **unverified**). The game's `driftAim` already reflects this: *Aphaenogaster senilis* split colonies differed within 5 days (bioRxiv 614719, from the existing note `queens-and-merging.md`; not re-read here).
- **A shared diet converges them, but not always.** A common diet diminished aggression between initially *moderately* aggressive Argentine-ant pairs; "initially highly aggressive colony pairs maintained high levels of injurious aggression throughout the study, independent of diet type" (Buczkowski et al. 2005, J Chem Ecol; abstract, **unverified**).
- **Drift between separated groups.** Six *Anoplolepis gracilipes* supercolonies had diverged: mutually aggressive pairs still shared 73.8% of their CHC compounds and 33.1% of their alleles; aggression was negatively correlated with CHC-profile similarity (Drescher et al. 2010, PLoS One; abstract, **unverified**). Divergence here is by genetic drift in groups whose gene flow is cut, not by fights.
- **Within a colony odour is homogenised**: a "gestalt" through the postpharyngeal gland, grooming, trophallaxis and nest material (Bos & d'Ettorre 2012, citing Soroker et al. 1994 and Lenoir et al.). The game's nest-odour drift toward the ants' mean (`nestDrift`) is this.
- **Population-level cue diversity is selected by parasites**: *Formica fusca* in Finland (high parasite pressure) had more C25 dimethylalkane isomers and good egg and nestmate discrimination; in UK populations (no social parasite) both were lost, though neutral gene diversity matched (Martin et al. 2011, Proc R Soc B, full text). A fungus is a parasite; an *evolving* odour diversity under fungal pressure is a possible later mechanic, not part of this epic.

**In the game:** divergence is by **separation**, and the game already has it for daughter colonies (`driftAim`, `ODOUR_DRIFT` 500 s, scalar). With K dimensions the aim is a **vector** at 1.5 tolerances from the mother, in a direction fixed by a hash of the seed and the daughter's site (pure, and the same on every load). An optional slow random walk of every colony's vector (Drescher 2010's drift) is **design** and left to S5. Convergence by shared food (B5 diet in `fungus-policy.md`) stays a fungus lever for later.

## 7. Supercolonies arise from lost cue diversity; kin neighbours are a real outcome, but a species trait

- **Bottleneck**: Argentine ants in the introduced range had lower genetic diversity, and "this loss is associated with reduced intraspecific aggression among spatially separate nests"; native populations were "more genetically variable and exhibit pronounced intraspecific aggression" (Tsutsui et al. 2000, PNAS). Individuals from less diverse colonies attacked those from more diverse ones, "attackers survived agonistic encounters more than six times as often as recipients of aggression" (Tsutsui et al. 2003, PNAS): selection removes the diverse.
- **Against bottleneck-only**: European supercolonies lost only limited neutral diversity, so "a genetic cleansing of recognition cues occurred after introduction"; one supercolony spans 6,000 km, the other is fixed for different recognition alleles with "extremely high" aggression between them (Giraud et al. 2002, PNAS). Native-range populations are *also* supercolonies, with relatedness not different from zero, 25-500 m across (Pedersen et al. 2006, Evolution; abstract, **unverified**). Borders between supercolonies are sharp and lethal: 16 contact zones, aggression and genetic distance correlated, geographic distance not (Thomas et al. 2006, Mol Ecol; abstract, **unverified**).
- **Against "supercolony is the norm in a dense population"**: *Brachymyrmex patagonicus* reaches a nest every 2.5 m yet keeps distinct colonies and strong aggression (Eyer et al. 2021; abstract).

**In the game:** a wild colony of an ordinary (multicolonial) species should usually be a stranger to its neighbours; kin neighbours are the supercolony case, a **species trait** (a wider tolerance: the neglectus note already holds this, `lasius-neglectus.md`), not an accident of the hash. At K = 3 and tolerance 0.35 two random colonies are kin with probability **0.116** (K = 1: 0.578; K = 2: 0.276; K = 4: 0.045), by the Monte Carlo. That leaves a small, real tail of kin neighbours, which the fungus can still work through, and no scripted odour assignment.

## 8. How many distinct odour identities does a local population hold?

- No source in this pass counts distinct CHC identities per area as a number. What the studies give: ten *Formica exsecta* colonies each with its own signature (Martin 2008); 14 colonies in 34 nests of acrobat ants on a set of olive trees (Frizzi 2015); a colony every 2.5 m in *B. patagonicus* (Eyer 2021); native Argentine-ant supercolonies a few hundred metres across (Pedersen 2006). So: in multicolonial species, tens of distinct identities within a few hundred metres, and nearly every neighbour a stranger.

**In the game:** the wild world's neighbours should be strangers nearly always (the Monte Carlo: **95.5%** of K = 3 wild colonies are strangers to *both* start colonies, against 30% now; a random greedy packing holds about **26** mutual strangers at K = 3, against 2.7 at K = 1). That is the "many identities" a map with dozens of colonies needs.

## What was not found

- A measurement that a colony's CHC profile moves *because of* aggressive contact, or away from a neighbour's with contact: not found (§5).
- The effective dimensionality of colony odour as a number, and how many identities a population holds as a number (§1, §8).
- Whether the per-compound effect is additive or interacting in a distance: only single-compound manipulations were found (Martin 2008, Guerrieri 2009, Neupert 2018).
- Langen et al. 2000, Reeve 1989, Sturgis & Gordon 2012/2013 and Martin & Drijfhout 2009 could not be read from here.

## The mechanic

Cheapest first. All steering and body: **no Jev situation field, legend or question changes** (odour never enters the question; `enemy` is an index into the situation, `situation.ts`).

| Cue (sensed) | Sense row | Response | Local rule | Off | Retires |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| contact: the other's odour vector against the perceiver's own | CHC (contact) | stranger or not (the existing `enemy`, fights, alarm) | `d = sqrt(sum w_k (o_k - t_k)^2) > tolerance` | K = 1: the scalar distance | nothing new; wild-site odour stays a hash (now K hashes) |
| contact that ended in a fight (design numbers) | CHC + encounter | the perceiver's foe template and a sharper boundary, per ant | store the aggressor's vector; strangers near it count extra; decays over minutes | `foeMemory` 0 | nothing; adds no global read |
| separation of a daughter or an isolated colony | CHC (nest gestalt) | drift of the nest odour vector | `driftAim` as a vector, hash-fixed direction | scalar `driftAim` | nothing |

**Stages** (PRD §91; beads under `ja-zg1u`):

- **S0, assays first, no behaviour change.** O1: the share of kin pairs among wild neighbours and the largest mutual-stranger set, at K = 1 (baseline) and K = 2-4 (target: K = 3 over 90% of wild colonies strangers to both start colonies; kin neighbours present but rare). O2: the invariant "a host is never a stranger to its own nest" at every K and every fungal share (today the proof is `MAX_MASK` x 0.5 = 0.3, which breaks in more dimensions: S2 caps the shift in the space instead). O3: a tick-cost bench for recognition. Goldens bit-exact.
- **S1, the refactor behind K = 1.** An odour vector per ant (K floats, interleaved), `nestOdour` a vector, one `odourDistance` in `recognition.ts` used by `battle.ts`, `sense.ts`, `attackCue.ts`, `fungus.ts`, `game.ts` (strategist, unity, raids, `rival`, `unity` measures). The recognition bands: `Recognition` keeps one `SumGrid` per band and a 32-bit `#mask`, `1 << b` (`BINS` 10, so at most 31 bands). K = 3 at 10 bands an axis is 1000 cells, so the bands become **sparse**: a map from a cell key to a `SumGrid`, created when an ant first lands in it, and the mask a list of the occupied strange cells. K = 1, BINS = 10 is the same set of cells in the same order, so goldens stay bit-exact. `vocab.ts`'s `look?(odour: number, tolerance)` takes the ant's slot instead. **SAVE_VERSION** 15 reads strictly (`game.ts`): the bump to 16 refuses old saves, as every bump has.
- **S2, K = 3 on (its own PR, goldens re-recorded and said so).** Start colonies on the main diagonal (so any two are strangers: distance `sqrt(K) x gap`); wild sites at `(hash01(seed, 101+k, rx, ry))` per axis, pure. **The fungus** keeps B3/B7, with its odour at the centre `(0.5, ...)`; a host's displacement is **capped in the space** (0.3), not a share of the distance, so the "never a stranger to its own nest" proof still holds; the odour weapon (`estrange`) pushes radially away from the fungal point. Unity takes longer the further the colonies sit from the fungal point (measured by an assay: time to unity in the Garden games, against PRD §22/§29/§37). The map's colour of an ant by odour (a hue today) needs a K = 3 readout (UI bead).
- **S3, the species weights** (`odourWeights`), default 1; the neglectus species keeps its wide tolerance.
- **S4, learned foe** behind `foeMemory` (off). **Gate:** the borderline-colony assay shows hysteresis (a pair that fought stays hostile at a gap where a pair that never met makes peace) without a runaway (no colony ends permanently at war with every neighbour, assay G and the long games).
- **S5, optional:** the slow random walk of colony vectors (Drescher 2010); strain-specific fungal odour (two strains smell different; **design**).
- **S6, Learn entry and the colour lens**, then strike the sense and gap rows.

## Play

- **See:** colonies in the endless world that are strangers to nearly everyone round them, so a walk of the map meets several distinct colours of ant, not two. A fight at a border leaves the fighting ants more hostile to that colour for some minutes (S4). Learn: a new entry under "Strangers and battles" saying what real ants do (a blend, a few axes that matter, a threshold) and how the game models it. The colour of a 3-D odour on the field is a design problem of its own (a hue and a second shade; a lens in Lab mode shows the three axes).
- **Choose:** the fungus's odour levers (B3 blend, B7 mask, calm, the odour weapon) now act over a distance: to unify two colonies the fungus must carry its odour into **both** and hold it long enough, so *which* colonies to work, and for how long, matters more. Nothing here is a lever on ants; the colonies' own odours are their nature (PRD §21).
- **Counterplay and progression:** the colonies push back as before (grooming and rejection). A weak start stays a slope: at the start there are two colonies and the distance between them is the same order as today; the wild neighbours are the new strangers, and the fungus meets them only as it spreads.
- **Cost:** no situation field changes; a cached game spends nothing as today; the hourly figure of every lane is unchanged (PRD §50.19, §54.4; the PR re-runs `npm run colony-cost` and `node scripts/fungus-cost.mjs` to say so). The cost that moves is CPU: recognition over more cells (S0's bench sets the budget, in ms).
- **Risks:**
  - *Permanent war* (too many strangers): every colony a stranger to every neighbour, nothing to ally, the fungus's unify route shut. Number to watch: the share of games that end unified in the Garden scenarios (PRD §22) against today's; the 0.116 kin share is the dial, and the tolerance can be raised.
  - *Unification starved*: the distance to the fungal point is larger in more dimensions (the host cap in S2); measured by time-to-unity.
  - *Cliff*: a foe memory that makes a border war permanent in a minute (S4's gate).
  - *Invisible*: three odour axes that the player cannot see; the lens and the Learn entry are in the plan, not an afterthought.
  - *Cost creep*: the sparse bands growing with continuous host odours; the bench catches it.
- **Owner decisions needed:** §Open questions.

## Open questions

1. **K and weights.** K = 3 (kin 0.116) or K = 2 (0.276) or K = 4 (0.045)? Sources give "a few" axes, no number (`OWNER` bead).
2. **Aggression-driven repulsion as the owner first put it.** Not supported by any source read, and Frizzi 2015 goes against it; the literature-grounded replacement is the learned foe (S4). If the owner still wants repulsion, it is **design** and must be marked so in the Learn entry (`OWNER` bead).
3. **Symmetric or one-sided distance.** Guerrieri 2009 for one-sided (novel cues rejected, absent ones not), Neupert 2018 against (a missing component also makes a non-nestmate). Symmetric is the default (`OWNER` bead).
4. **Are kin neighbours acceptable** for an ordinary species (about 12% of wild pairs at K = 3), or should wild odours be chosen by rejection against their neighbours' (`ja-s37s` option c, still pure since the neighbours' odours are pure)? Recommendation: accept, and measure in E6.
5. **To measure:** the S0 assays; the kin share against the real neighbours the endless world makes (not uniform draws); the bench.
