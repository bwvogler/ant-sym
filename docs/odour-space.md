The owner asked (2026-11-25) to "widen odour space and drive scents away when there is aggressive contact, or some other mechanic grounded in literature; more dimensions makes sense". Today a colony's odour is one number in [0, 1] and two ants are strangers when their odours are further apart than 0.35 (`TOLERANCE`, `recognition.ts`). Measured by a 200,000-draw Monte Carlo over uniform odours (scratch script, not in the repo): two random colonies are kin with probability **0.578**, a wild colony (hash odour) is a stranger to both start colonies (at 0 and 1) only **30%** of the time, and the largest set of mutual strangers is 3 colonies (0, 0.5, 1; a random packing gives 2.5-2.7). The question here: *how many things does a real colony's odour have, how do real ants judge a difference, and does contact with a rival change either?*

## 1. A colony's odour is a blend of cuticular hydrocarbons, a point in a many-dimensional space

- Recognition cues are cuticular hydrocarbons (CHCs): alkanes, methyl-branched alkanes and alkenes. Bos & d'Ettorre 2012 describe the label as "multicomponent". Esponda & Gordon 2015 give the geometry the literature uses: "CHC-space, an n-dimensional Euclidean vector space in which each coordinate axis represents the concentration of a given hydrocarbon ... a particular chemical profile is a point in this multi-dimensional space", and the template-similarity model "describes recognition as a function of the distance between an individual's internal template ... and the cues that individual detects".
- **Against "many equal dimensions":** the space is large but few directions in it do the work.
  - *Formica exsecta*: ten colonies whose profiles are, in the paper's words, ">95% a C23-C29 alkane and a Z9-alkene homologous series"; aggression was elicited by the alkene part, and changing the abundance of a single Z9-alkene on a live ant raised the aggression it got from nestmates, pooled over five colonies (the colonies did not differ, Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.1; no per-colony test is reported). The authors caution that the alkanes may still enhance the Z9-alkene effect. They conclude recognition "has evolved to rely upon highly sensitive responses to relatively few compounds" (Martin et al. 2008, Proc R Soc B).
  - Argentine ants: homologous compounds (same methyl position, different chain length) occur in correlated concentrations and "convey the same information" (van Wilgenburg et al. 2010, J Chem Ecol).
  - *Brachymyrmex patagonicus*: aggression between colonies tracked their genetic difference but not their overall chemical differentiation, "which may suggest that only a few chemical compounds influence nestmate recognition in this species **or that weak chemical differences are sufficient to elicit aggression**" (Eyer et al. 2021, Ecol Evol; PMC full text read by the reviewer, abstract sentence re-checked by the author). The second reading does not support "few axes", so this is the weakest of the three.
- No paper in this pass gives a number for the effective dimensionality of a colony's odour *for recognition*. "A few" is the reading of these three. Three things from 2026-11-28 bear on the count of axes, none of them a recognition dimensionality:
  - In the desert ant *Cataglyphis niger* (47 colonies, six workers each, 34 CHCs measured), the principal components of the CHC matrix that explained at least 4% of the variation were **the first six**, at the individual and at the colony level, and PC4 and PC5 together explained less than 13% (Cohen et al. 2025, Ecol Evol; passages read in the open text: the number of PCs above a 4% cut-off is the authors' analysis choice, and the PCs are about chemical variation, not about what ants discriminate). The authors read the first PCs as largely environmental, not genetic.
  - Hydrocarbons are not independent: strong genetic correlations between compounds are expected because they share biosynthetic pathways (Walsh et al. 2020, Proc R Soc B, *Monomorium pharaonis*; abstract, **unverified**; no number taken), which fits "fewer effective axes than compounds" and gives no count.
  - Across 78 ant species almost 1,000 CHCs fall into 187 homologous series and **ten hydrocarbon groups** (Martin & Drijfhout 2009; abstract, **unverified**): a count of classes in a compilation of species, not of what one colony's odour varies in. They add that the dimethylalkanes are "likely candidates for species and nest-mate discrimination signals".
  So the K = 4 of the owner's decision sits inside the "a few" range these give (four classes of compound against six PCs above 4% in one species), and nothing found contradicts it; nothing found supports 4 over 3 or 6 either, which is why it stays **design**.

**In the game:** the odour becomes a short vector, **K = 4** (decided by the owner, 2026-11-25; the number is **design**, not a measured dimensionality, §1 above; K = 3 was considered, see "Decided"), one axis for a *class* of compounds (say the dominant alkene, the methyl-branched series, the diet-borne part), not for single hydrocarbons. Each axis has a **weight** (default 1), a per-species trait, so a species can recognise on one axis only (Martin 2008) without the code changing. K = 1 with weight 1 is today's game, bit for bit (the "off" setting). The sense is the existing one (vision.md, "Cuticular hydrocarbons (contact)"): no new sense row.

## 2. Similarity is a template-to-label distance against a threshold, not an identity match

- Ants compare the label on another's cuticle with a neural template of their own colony's; "typically, ant workers attack the encountered individuals when the perceived differential exceeds a certain threshold; below this threshold, the encountered individual is accepted or simply ignored" (Bos & d'Ettorre 2012, citing Sherman et al. 1997 and van Zweden & d'Ettorre 2010). Reeve 1989's acceptance-threshold model is the origin of this reading (title checked in Crossref, not read).
- The colony template is not fixed: it is held in the central nervous system and adjusts slowly (more than 2 h after a workers' antennae were loaded with a foreign gland extract, 15 h to accept it) without losing the old label as nestmate (Leonhardt et al. 2007, J Comp Physiol A; abstract, **unverified**).
- **The distance is not obviously symmetric.**
  - For: *Camponotus herculeanus* rejected nestmates bearing an *added* dimethyl alkane and did not reject ants lacking one; "they do not specifically recognize nest-mates, but rather recognize and reject non-nest-mates bearing odour cues that are novel to their own colony" (Guerrieri et al. 2009, Proc R Soc B; full text read by the reviewer of #432).
  - Against: in *C. floridanus*, sub-colonies given one or two extra compounds learned them as nestmate labels, and a worker from the two-compound sub-colony attacked ants with only one, so "additional components as well as the lack of a component can alter a label as non-nestmate" (Neupert et al. 2018, Front Behav Neurosci; full text read by the reviewer of #432). That paper also finds ants hold **several** templates and accept both the old and the new label.

**In the game:** a contact is read as a **distance in the K-space** from the perceiver's own odour (its template: today's code already compares the other ant's band centre with the perceiver's own odour, `battle.ts`) to the other's, `d = sqrt(sum_k w_k (o_k - t_k)^2)`, and the other is a stranger when `d > tolerance + STRANGER_EPS`. The tolerance stays 0.35 and stays per ant (the fungus's B4 lever widens it). A **one-sided** distance (only the perceiver's missing dimensions count) is possible and would give directed aggression, which `contact-actions.md` already models; the sources split on it, so it is left to an owner decision (§ Open questions). Default: symmetric.

## 3. Recognition is distributed: each ant has its own boundary, and experience moves it

- Esponda & Gordon 2015 (full text) model recognition like the vertebrate immune system: "no single ant knows the odour identity of the colony ... Each ant responds to the odour of other ants by reference to its own unique decision boundary, which is a result of its experience of encounters with other ants". In the model, "ants habituate to an odour if each encounter with the odour is not associated with aggression, or if there is a positive experience such as trophallaxis or grooming, while aggression or the absence of a positive experience leads to gradual sensitization to non-nestmate odours." It is a model: its predictions (variable assays, dependence on the number of ants tested, change over time and with experience) are what the authors say the earlier experiments show.
- Counter-case from the same paper: *Cataglyphis niger* reduced its aggression to a particular non-nestmate over successive encounters (Nowbahari, cited there; not read).
- Task matters: exterior workers are more aggressive than interior ones, and recognition is more likely towards neighbours' ants (Sturgis & Gordon 2013, as Esponda & Gordon cite it; not read).

**In the game:** the game already has a per-ant tolerance array (`Colony.tolerance[i]`), set from the colony's and moved by the fungus. Habituation and sensitisation are a **per-ant, perceiver-side** change to that boundary, the cue is "a contact that ended in a fight" (sensitises) or "in calm" (habituates), both local. This is the home of the owner's "aggressive contact changes things" (§5), not the colony's smell.

## 4. Neighbours: "nasty neighbour" is common, "dear enemy" is not what most ants show, and neither is an odour shift

- ***Pogonomyrmex barbatus***: "encounters on foraging trails with ants from neighboring colonies deter foraging more than encounters with ants from distant ones" (Gordon 1989, Oecologia; abstract, **unverified**): neighbours are distinguished from strangers.
- ***Oecophylla smaragdina***: colonies recognised a greater share of neighbours' workers as non-members, and showed more aggression to them once recognised, "evidence that differential levels of aggression involve both a perceptual and a behavioral component". No rapid learning of new colonies in 10 days of contact. "The nasty neighbor effect may be much more common than the dear enemy effect among group-living species" (Newey et al. 2010, Ecology; abstract, **unverified**).
- ***Crematogaster scutellaris***: read in full. 34 nests fell into 14 genetic colonies; CHC profiles differed between colonies; "aggressiveness increased with both spatial proximity and similarity in cuticular hydrocarbon profiles among colonies, suggesting a nasty neighbor effect"; chemical distance was not correlated with spatial distance (Mantel r = -0.049, P = 0.73) nor with genetic distance, and the authors write that CHC profiles "may be linked to external environmental factors rather than genetic relationships" (Frizzi et al. 2015, PLoS One); the Mantel result is a non-correlation, not a positive finding for the environment. **This goes against the idea that neighbours' odours move apart**: the neighbours that fought most were the chemically *closest*.
- ***Myrmica* (Fürst et al. 2012, Proc R Soc B; PMC full text, read by the reviewer and its results sentences re-checked by the author):** aggression rose with **geographic distance only**; "genetic and chemical distances do not explain aggression". The abstract's "increases with distance (geographical, genetic and chemical)" is the hypothesis, not the result. The paper does not mention dear enemy, so it is **not** a dear-enemy example here; it is the authors' test of the adjustable-threshold model, and it too shows no chemical distance effect.
- **Against "nasty neighbour" as the rule:** around supercolonies of the fire ant *Solenopsis saevissima*, eight *other* ant species living beside its nests (an interspecific test, not colonies of one species) showed both the dear-enemy and the nasty-neighbour effect (Roux et al. 2013, Behav Processes; abstract, **unverified**; the previous text of this note called it fire-ant neighbours, which it is not); two *Pheidole* species showed habituation and dear enemy (Langen et al. 2000, cited by Esponda & Gordon; **not read**).

**In the game:** who is a neighbour is already local (a contact at the border). The neighbour effect is a **perceiver** effect (§5), and the game may show nasty neighbours (the commoner finding) without any change to colony odour. Dear-enemy is the habituation half of §3 and is what the fungus's calming levers already buy.

## 5. Aggression teaches the perceiver an enemy template; the sources do not show it moving the colony's odour

- **Bey et al. 2025 (Curr Biol 35:407-12; design read from the bioRxiv preprint v1, main text and methods in full; supplementary tables, the figures' data and the published text not reached: numbers other than the ones below are unverified).** Published abstract: "associative learning plays a crucial role in the formation of both nestmate and non-nestmate recognition templates and ... the aggression received by an ant acts as an unconditioned stimulus that the ant likely associates with the odor label of its enemy". The design, one species (*Lasius niger*, 47 colonies across four experiments, 2016-2021, each experiment on its own colonies, collected in the field and tested within three months), single ants in a small arena, aggression scored by an observer blind to treatment (index 0-4: bump 1, open mandibles 2, bite 3, gaster-bend 4, weighted by duration):
  - **Experiment 1:** colonies within foraging distance were more aggressive to each other than colonies further apart (a nasty-neighbour effect in *L. niger*; Supplement 1, not seen).
  - **Experiment 2 (five days):** a focal ant met a *different individual* of one non-nestmate colony for at most **1 minute once a day for five days** (an encounter was stopped at gaster-bending, to prevent a fight to death); on day six it met a non-nestmate from the *same* colony ("known"), from another colony ("unknown") or a nestmate. Known non-nestmates drew the most aggression (n = 312, treatment p < 0.001; n = 104 encounters per combination); unknown ones drew no more than ants trained on nestmates only (p = 0.74); nestmates drew none whatever the training (n = 156, p = 0.25). Over the five training days the aggression of ants meeting non-nestmates rose; ants meeting nestmates stayed peaceful. 468 focal ants in all (2 focal colonies x 3 training x 3 test opponents x 26). **The memory had lasted at least the 24 h between the last training day and the test**; nothing longer was tested.
  - **Experiment 3 (45 minutes):** three 1-minute encounters, 15 minutes apart, then the test on the fourth encounter (n = 94: 33 known, 30 unknown, 31 nestmate). Known beat nestmate (p < 0.05) but known against unknown was only a trend (p = 0.057), and unknown colonies drew intermediate aggression, which the authors read as a colony-independent rise of aggression within the hour (they suggest biogenic amines; it is not measured here) that had gone by the multi-day design. No rise over the three training encounters (Suppl., not seen). So the learning is **weaker and less colony-specific within 45 minutes than over days**.
  - **Experiment 4 (aggression as the unconditioned stimulus):** three daily 1-minute trainings with non-nestmates that were either aggressive or **made passive by cutting their antennae**; test on day four with known or unknown aggressive non-nestmates (n = 322, 3 focal colonies x 2 opponent colonies x 4 treatments x 13-15). Known colony: more aggression if it had been aggressive in training than if it had been passive (p < 0.05); unknown colony: no difference (p = 0.50). **There was no nestmate-only control in this experiment**, so "became less aggressive after meeting passive members" is relative to ants that had been attacked, not to a naive ant (the earlier wording of this note read it as a fall below baseline); the authors also note the passive ants were disoriented, a difference from aggressive ants that is not only aggression.
  - **Effect sizes:** the text gives p-values and group sizes, no means or standard deviations (those are in figures and supplementary tables, which were not reached): **no effect size can be quoted** here.
  - **Is it per-ant perceiver learning, as this note claims?** Yes in design: each focal ant was trained and tested alone, met a new individual every day, and was scored on its own aggression, and the specificity (known against unknown colony) rules out a pure arousal reading in experiment 2. The authors' reading is "most likely" a learned label (the conditioned stimulus is "likely" the colony odour); they did not measure the odour, the ant's template or any change in the *opponents'* odour. They do not test generalisation: only the same colony against one other colony, never an odour *near* the trained one, so "strangers close to the foe template count extra" (below) has no support in this paper. They propose, and do not test, that it explains nasty neighbours in the field. They also state that ants' individual experience makes recognition vary between nestmates and that colony-level decisions integrate them, the distributed model of Esponda & Gordon 2015.
- **A disagreement on timescale and setting, not a settled counter-case:** Newey et al. 2010 found no improvement in weaver ants' recognition of *unfamiliar* colonies after 10 days of contact (neighbours against strangers; abstract, **unverified**), where Bey et al. report learning within five brief lab encounters (and a weaker, partly non-specific effect within 45 minutes). Different species (a weaver ant with rare intruders against a *Lasius* from a nasty-neighbour population), setting (a colony's workers in 10 days of contact against single ants trained one at a time) and question; the note cannot reconcile them. Newey's own abstract says its result contrasts with "other species in which rapid learning of the identity of new potential enemies has been demonstrated", so the learned foe is a trait some species have, which is another reason it stays a species-level setting if it is built.
- Neupert et al. 2018: ants learn several labels as nestmate; Leonhardt et al. 2007: templates adjust in hours.
- **Searched for, not found (so far):** a measurement that a *colony's* CHC profile shifts because it was in fights, or that two neighbouring colonies' profiles move away from each other with contact. Scope: web and PMC searches by two sessions ("ant cuticular hydrocarbons change after fighting", "character displacement cuticular hydrocarbons ants", "divergence between neighbouring colonies"), and on 2026-11-28 Europe PMC full-index searches (aggression or fight with CHC change; neighbour with divergence, convergence or character displacement; contact transfer; chemical against spatial distance; recognition and principal components): nothing that fits turned up. Europe PMC and Crossref were reachable on 2026-11-28 (PubMed's own pages and several publisher and repository hosts were not), but a title-and-abstract search is not exhaustive: the claim stays "not found", not "shown absent". The chemical-divergence mechanisms found are in §6.
- **Limits on that claim (found by the reviewer; on 2026-11-28 one was read in full, the rest by abstract, below):**
  - *Polyergus breviceps* queens that kill a *Formica* host queen take on a profile "virtually identical" to the victim's, shifting "from primarily normal alkanes to methyl and di-methyl branched alkanes after attacks" (Johnson, Vander Meer & Lavine 2001, J Chem Ecol 27:1787-1804, PubMed 11545371; **abstract read 2026-11-28, full text not reached: unverified**; the previous text of this note named the authors wrongly). The authors suggest compounds "were transferred to the parasite queen during their aggressive interaction". It is an individual, one-way, parasite-host transfer from the victim to the *attacker*, but it is aggression changing a scent.
  - Cue transfer by contact and nest material, **read in full**: *Camponotus aethiops* ants housed on another colony's nest soil for 24 h had a median transmission index of 0.13 over 36 CHC peaks, "around 13% more similar" in relative abundance to the colony that supplied the soil; the index was positive for 29 of 36 peaks (binomial p = 0.005). Target ants housed on the focal colony's own soil received less aggression from that colony (t155 = 2.75, p = 0.007), but the reverse test (the focal ant given the other colony's soil and meeting that colony) was not significant across the five pairs (p = 0.29), so the template moved in some pairs only. Three peaks rose in every soil-treated ant whatever the colony, which the authors read as a physiological response to non-nestmate cues. Across the 20 treatment-colony-experiment combinations Euclidean chemical distance did not predict aggression (p > 0.1) (Bos, Grinsted & Holman 2011, "Wax On, Wax Off", PLoS One 6:e19435). This is convergence through a shared material, a contact effect *without* aggression, so it supports "odours converge on a shared source", and the Euclidean-distance null is a caution for the K-space distance of §2 (one species, 20 points, so weak). Argentine ants given brief contact (as short as 2 min) with a cockroach carrying very-long-chain hydrocarbons were attacked by their nestmates, and ants given an extract of those hydrocarbons likewise (Liang, Blomquist & Silverman 2001, Comp Biochem Physiol B 129:871-82; **abstract read, unverified**; the previous text called this "Liang & Silverman 2001"). A field diet experiment on the same species found no prey hydrocarbons transferred to ants in any field treatment and aggressive supercolonies stayed aggressive; only an extreme lab diet gave some transfer (van Wilgenburg, Mariotta & Tsutsui 2022, Insects 13:335; abstract, **unverified**), which goes against reading diet convergence as a field mechanism (§6).
  - Character displacement appears in a 2020 Myrmecological News review as selection by parasites ("chemical insignificance"): evolutionary, not within-game timescale (not read).

**Verdict on the owner's first idea ("drive scents away on aggressive contact")**: **not supported by anything found, within the limits above.** What the sources do support for the same visible outcome (a war that leaves the two sides *less* able to make peace) is a change in the **perceiver**: an enemy template learned from aggression and a boundary that sensitises (§3, Bey 2025, Esponda & Gordon 2015). The evidence that neighbours' *scents* diverge by contact is absent, and Frizzi et al. 2015 (closest colonies fight most) and the diet work in §6 (shared environment converges scents) lean the other way.

**In the game:** a **learned foe**, per ant: when an ant is attacked by (or fights) a stranger, it stores that stranger's odour vector as a foe template, with a strength that decays over minutes; strangers close to the foe template then count with extra weight (and the borderline "near kin" lose their benefit of the doubt). It is perceiver state, local, and needs no global read. All numbers are **design**; the sign and the mechanism are from Bey 2025 (preprint read in full on 2026-11-28) and Esponda & Gordon 2015, and the evidence is **one laboratory paper on one species** against a field null on a longer timescale (Newey 2010; Argentine-ant fights dissolving after a shared diet: §6), which is why it is **staged last, off by default** (§ The mechanic, S4).

**What reading Bey 2025 changes in this design (2026-11-28; for the owner):**
- *Per ant, not per colony:* confirmed by the design (each focal ant trained and tested alone). Nothing in it is colony-level.
- *Timescale:* the only memory span tested is **at least 24 h** (experiment 2; experiment 4 also tests the day after training). "Decays over minutes" has **no support** in it: the nearest thing to a short timescale is a weaker, partly colony-independent rise of aggression within 45 minutes (experiment 3). Whatever decay S4 uses is **design**, and game-time minutes are a compression of a day-scale lab result, not a finding. The paper measures no decay curve.
- *Strength:* the paper gives no effect size in its text (p-values only); S4's numbers cannot be taken from it, only the sign (more aggression to the trained colony, none to others).
- *Dose:* three 1-minute encounters were enough for a trend in 45 minutes (p = 0.057 known against unknown) and five daily encounters for a clear effect; an ant attacked once is not tested. A learned foe that forms on a single attack is therefore **design**, not the paper's finding.
- *Specificity:* the effect was specific to the trained colony; generalisation to near odours was not tested, so "strangers close to the foe template count extra" is **design** (it follows from the template-distance model, not from an experiment).
- *A second, colony-independent term* (a short rise of aggression after fights, which the authors suggest is a general arousal effect) is a possible, optional addition for S4, **design**.
None of this changes the sign, the perceiver-side placement or the off-by-default staging, and nothing in it argues for explicit repulsion of odours.

## 6. What does move a colony's odour: separation, diet and the shared environment, over days to weeks

- **Isolation plus different food diverges odours.** Argentine-ant colony fragments, isolated and fed prey with different CHCs, attacked their former nestmates; isolation of 28 days or more kept them apart for at least 28 days more after a bridge, while fragments isolated less than 28 days reunited (Silverman & Liang 2001, Naturwissenschaften; abstract, **unverified**). The game's `driftAim` already reflects this: *Aphaenogaster senilis* split colonies differed within 5 days (bioRxiv 614719, from the existing note `queens-and-merging.md`; not re-read here).
- **A shared diet converges them, but not always.** A common diet diminished aggression between initially *moderately* aggressive Argentine-ant pairs; "initially highly aggressive colony pairs maintained high levels of injurious aggression throughout the study, independent of diet type" (Buczkowski et al. 2005, J Chem Ecol; abstract, **unverified**). **Against diet convergence as a field mechanism:** in a field and a lab diet experiment on the same species aggressive supercolonies stayed aggressive after a month whatever the diet and no prey hydrocarbons were found on field-fed ants (van Wilgenburg et al. 2022, Insects; abstract, **unverified**), and at 16 supercolony borders aggression tracked genetic differentiation, which the authors take as inconsistent with environmentally derived cues being the prime determinant in the field (Thomas et al. 2006, abstract, **unverified**). The diet lever is therefore a laboratory result with a field null.
- **Drift between separated groups.** Six *Anoplolepis gracilipes* supercolonies had diverged: mutually aggressive pairs still shared 73.8% of their CHC compounds and 33.1% of their alleles; aggression was negatively correlated with CHC-profile similarity (Drescher et al. 2010, PLoS One; full text read by the reviewer of #432, who notes 220 vs 215 genotyped ants and one CHC correlation labelled inconsistently in its table and text: not material). Divergence here is by genetic drift in groups whose gene flow is cut, not by fights.
- **Within a colony odour is homogenised**: a "gestalt" through the postpharyngeal gland, grooming, trophallaxis and nest material (Bos & d'Ettorre 2012, citing Soroker et al. 1994 and Lenoir et al.). The game's nest-odour drift toward the ants' mean (`nestDrift`) is this.
- **Population-level cue diversity is selected by parasites**: *Formica fusca* in Finland (high parasite pressure) had more C25 dimethylalkane isomers and good egg and nestmate discrimination; in UK populations (no social parasite) both were lost, though neutral gene diversity matched (Martin et al. 2011, Proc R Soc B, full text). A fungus is a parasite; an *evolving* odour diversity under fungal pressure is a possible later mechanic, not part of this epic.
- **A fungal parasite changes the host's odour and the host's template** (found 2026-11-28; abstract only, **unverified**, a different fungus from the game's): ants infected by *Rickia wasmannii* (an ant-parasitic fungus of *Myrmica*) consistently show more of the alkane n-C23, infected ants were more peaceful to infected but not to uninfected non-nestmates, and adding synthetic n-C23 to corpses reduced aggression in uninfected ants (Csata et al. 2023, Commun Biol). One compound acting as a "pacifying" signal also fits "few axes" (§1). Perceiver plasticity at the receptor level is reported too: leaf-cutting ants exposed to a non-nestmate odour were less aggressive to it and the antennal odorant-receptor profiles of two colonies exposed to the same odour converged (Bey et al. 2026, iScience; abstract, **unverified**). Neither is built on here.

**In the game:** divergence is by **separation**, and the game already has it for daughter colonies (`driftAim`, `ODOUR_DRIFT` 500 s, scalar). With K dimensions the aim is a **vector** at 1.5 tolerances from the mother, in a direction fixed by a hash of the seed and the daughter's site (pure, and the same on every load). An optional slow random walk of every colony's vector (Drescher 2010's drift) is **design** and left to S5. Convergence by shared food (B5 diet in `fungus-policy.md`) stays a fungus lever for later.

## 7. Supercolonies are proposed to arise from lost cue diversity; kin neighbours are a real outcome, but a species trait

- **Bottleneck** (the authors' inference from neutral markers; the recognition cues themselves were not measured): Argentine ants in the introduced range had lower genetic diversity, and "this loss is associated with reduced intraspecific aggression among spatially separate nests"; native populations were "more genetically variable and exhibit pronounced intraspecific aggression" (Tsutsui et al. 2000, PNAS; correlational: 8 native against 7 introduced populations). Individuals from less diverse colonies attacked those from more diverse ones, "attackers survived agonistic encounters more than six times as often as recipients of aggression" (Tsutsui et al. 2003, PNAS). The "six times" is a ratio among trials with exactly one survivor (59% of trials), not a per-encounter rate, and the less-diverse-attack-more result holds for polymorphic loci and alleles but **not** for heterozygosity (P = 0.377).
- **Against bottleneck-only**: European supercolonies lost only limited neutral diversity (Giraud et al. genotyped neutral microsatellites only), so the authors "suggest" a "genetic cleansing" of recognition cues after introduction (an inference; only those two words are theirs here); one supercolony spans 6,000 km, and the other is inferred to be fixed for different recognition alleles, with "extremely high" aggression between them (Giraud et al. 2002, PNAS). Native-range populations are *also* supercolonies, with relatedness not different from zero, 25-500 m across (Pedersen et al. 2006, Evolution; abstract, **unverified**). Borders between supercolonies are sharp and lethal: 16 contact zones, aggression and genetic distance correlated, geographic distance not (Thomas et al. 2006, Mol Ecol; abstract, **unverified**).
- **Against "supercolony is the norm in a dense population"**: *Brachymyrmex patagonicus* reaches a colony every 2.5 m on one property (3.2 m on the other) yet keeps distinct colonies and strong aggression (Eyer et al. 2021): "a different colony ... every 3.2 m" on one property and every 2.5 m on another.

**In the game:** a wild colony of an ordinary (multicolonial) species should usually be a stranger to its neighbours; kin neighbours are the supercolony case, a **species trait** (a wider tolerance: the neglectus note already holds this, `lasius-neglectus.md`), not an accident of the hash. At K = 4 and tolerance 0.35 two random colonies are kin with probability about **0.044** (K = 1: 0.578; K = 2: 0.278, exact 0.27801; K = 3, considered: 0.117), by the Monte Carlo (the reviewer's 4,000,000-draw rerun gave 0.0440 at K = 4). That leaves a very small tail of kin neighbours, which the fungus can still work through, and no scripted odour assignment.

## 8. How many distinct odour identities does a local population hold?

- No source in this pass counts distinct CHC identities per area as a number. What the studies give: ten *Formica exsecta* colonies each with its own signature (Martin 2008); 14 colonies in 34 nests of acrobat ants on a set of olive trees (Frizzi 2015); a colony every 2.5 m on one property (3.2 m on another) in *B. patagonicus* (Eyer 2021); native Argentine-ant supercolonies a few hundred metres across (Pedersen 2006). So: in multicolonial species, tens of distinct identities within a few hundred metres, and nearly every neighbour a stranger. Searched again on 2026-11-28 (Europe PMC, title and abstract): no count of CHC identities per area, and no effective dimensionality, was found; Cohen et al. 2025 (*C. niger*, 47 colonies) is the only one with colony-level CHC principal components (six above 4%, §1), and it is not an identity count.

**In the game:** the wild world's neighbours should be strangers nearly always (the Monte Carlo: **99%** of K = 4 wild colonies are strangers to *both* start colonies, against 30% now (K = 3, considered: 95.5%); a random greedy packing holds about **78** mutual strangers at K = 4 (the note's own figure, which the reviewer did not re-run; K = 3: about 26, re-run 26.4), against 2.5-2.7 at K = 1 (the maximum is 3)). That is the "many identities" a map with dozens of colonies needs.

## What was not found

- A measurement that a colony's CHC profile moves *because of* aggressive contact, or away from a neighbour's with contact: not found (§5; searched again on Europe PMC on 2026-11-28). What was found is the opposite direction or a different cause: a victim's profile transferred to the *attacker* in one parasite-host kill (Johnson et al. 2001, abstract), convergence through a shared nest soil without any fight (Bos et al. 2011, read), and ordinary drift and diet (§6).
- The effective dimensionality of colony odour *for recognition* as a number: not found (§1). The nearest numbers are the six principal components above 4% of the CHC variation in one species (Cohen et al. 2025) and ten hydrocarbon classes across 78 species (Martin & Drijfhout 2009, abstract): neither is that number.

## The mechanic

Cheapest first. All steering and body: **no Jev situation field, legend or question changes** (odour never enters the question; `enemy` is an index into the situation, `situation.ts`).

| Cue (sensed) | Sense row | Response | Local rule | Off | Retires |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| contact: the other's odour vector against the perceiver's own | CHC (contact) | stranger or not (the existing `enemy`, fights, alarm) | `d = sqrt(sum w_k (o_k - t_k)^2) > tolerance` | K = 1: the scalar distance | nothing new; wild-site odour stays a hash (now K hashes) |
| contact that ended in a fight (design numbers) | CHC + encounter | the perceiver's foe template and a sharper boundary, per ant | store the aggressor's vector; strangers near it count extra; decays over minutes | `foeMemory` 0 | nothing; adds no global read |
| separation of a daughter or an isolated colony | CHC (nest gestalt) | drift of the nest odour vector | `driftAim` as a vector, hash-fixed direction | scalar `driftAim` | nothing |

**Stages** (PRD §91; beads under `ja-zg1u`):

- **S0, assays first, no behaviour change.** O1: the share of kin pairs among wild neighbours and the largest mutual-stranger set, at K = 1 (baseline) and K = 2-4 (target: K = 4 over 95% of wild colonies strangers to both start colonies; kin neighbours rare). O2: the invariant "a host is never a stranger to its own nest" at every K and every fungal share (today the proof is `MAX_MASK` x 0.5 = 0.3, which breaks in more dimensions: S2 caps the shift in the space instead). O3: a tick-cost bench for recognition. Goldens bit-exact.
- **S1, the refactor behind K = 1 (built, `ja-zg1u.3`; what was built and the O3 figures are in PRD §91).** An odour vector per ant (K floats, interleaved), `nestOdour` a vector, one `odourDistance` in `recognition.ts` used by `battle.ts`, `sense.ts`, `attackCue.ts`, `fungus.ts`, `game.ts` (strategist, unity, raids, `rival`, `unity` measures). The recognition bands: `Recognition` keeps one `SumGrid` per band and a 32-bit `#mask`, `1 << b` (`BINS` 10, so at most 31 bands). K = 4 at 10 bands an axis is 10^4 cells per band set (K = 3 would be 1000), so the bands become **sparse**: a map from a cell key to a `SumGrid`, created when an ant first lands in it, and the mask a list of the occupied strange cells. K = 1, BINS = 10 is the same set of cells in the same order, so goldens stay bit-exact. `vocab.ts`'s `look?(odour: number, tolerance)` takes the ant's slot instead. **SAVE_VERSION** 15 reads strictly (`game.ts`): the bump to 16 refuses old saves, as every bump has.
- **S2, K = 4 on (built, `ja-zg1u.4`; what was built and measured is in PRD §91; its own PR; the goldens did not move, so none was re-recorded).** Start colonies on the main diagonal (so any two are strangers: distance `sqrt(K) x gap`); wild sites at `hash01(seed, odourSalt(k), rx, ry)` per axis, pure (salt 101 for axis 0, 201 up for the rest). **The fungus** keeps B3/B7, with its odour at the centre `(0.5, ...)`; a host's displacement is **capped in the space** at `hostCap(K) = tolerance - sqrt(K) / (2 BINS)` (0.25 at K = 4: the band rounding counted, not the bare 0.3 first planned), not a share of the distance, so the "never a stranger to its own nest" proof still holds; the odour weapon (`estrange`) pushes 0.5 along the line from the nest through the fungal point and past it (away from the nest's own odour: the S1 summary's "away from the fungal point" was a slip); a daughter drifts 1.5 tolerances along a hash-fixed direction; the neglectus species' tolerance is the space's diameter (`neverStranger`). Unity takes longer the further the colonies sit from the fungal point (measured by an assay: time to unity in the Garden games, against PRD §22/§29/§37). The map's colour of an ant by odour (a hue today) needs a K = 4 readout (UI bead).
- **S3, the species weights (built, `ja-zg1u.5`; what was built and checked is in PRD §91).** `ColonyParams.odourWeights`, one number per axis, absent meaning all 1: `d = sqrt(sum w_k (a_k - b_k)^2)`, measured by the **perceiver**, in every consumer of the distance (fights by band, by distance and by contact, the attack cue, an ant's look at enemies, the fungus's touch, the queen's guard, raids, unity's mixing and allied contact). **Weights are held to 0..1**: the weighted distance then never exceeds the plain one, so the own-nest cap (`hostCap`, derived for the plain distance) and the diameter bound still hold without a new derivation, and a weight above 1 is only a smaller tolerance in disguise. The space's diameter under weights is `sqrt(sum w)`: the neglectus species' tolerance and unity's recognition share use it. A daughter's drift is taken in the weighted metric, over the axes the species smells (a zero-weight axis does not move). Martin et al. 2008 (§1: recognition rests on a few compounds) is the reason for the trait; no species is given weights yet (it would be design, not a measurement), the one-axis species of the tests stands for it. Weights are species config, derived at construction (`Colony.odourW`, left out of the save).
- **S4, learned foe** behind `foeMemory` (off). **Gate:** the borderline-colony assay shows hysteresis (a pair that fought stays hostile at a gap where a pair that never met makes peace) without a runaway (no colony ends permanently at war with every neighbour, assay G and the long games).
- **S5, optional:** the slow random walk of colony vectors (Drescher 2010); strain-specific fungal odour (two strains smell different; **design**).
- **S6, Learn entry and the colour lens**, then strike the sense and gap rows.

## Play

- **See:** colonies in the endless world that are strangers to nearly everyone round them, so a walk of the map meets several distinct colours of ant, not two. A fight at a border leaves the fighting ants more hostile to that colour for some minutes (S4). Learn: a new entry under "Strangers and battles" saying what real ants do (a blend, a few axes that matter, a threshold) and how the game models it. The colour of a 3-D odour on the field is a design problem of its own (a hue and a second shade; a lens in Lab mode shows the four axes).
- **Choose:** the fungus's odour levers (B3 blend, B7 mask, calm, the odour weapon) now act over a distance: to unify two colonies the fungus must carry its odour into **both** and hold it long enough, so *which* colonies to work, and for how long, matters more. Nothing here is a lever on ants; the colonies' own odours are their nature (PRD §21).
- **Counterplay and progression:** the colonies push back as before (grooming and rejection). A weak start stays a slope: at the start there are two colonies and the distance between them is the same order as today; the wild neighbours are the new strangers, and the fungus meets them only as it spreads.
- **Cost:** no situation field changes; a cached game spends nothing as today; the hourly figure of every lane is unchanged (PRD §50.19, §54.4; the PR re-runs `npm run colony-cost` and `node scripts/fungus-cost.mjs` to say so). The cost that moves is CPU: recognition over more cells (S0's bench sets the budget, in ms).
- **Risks:**
  - *Permanent war* (too many strangers): every colony a stranger to every neighbour, nothing to ally, the fungus's unify route shut. Number to watch: the share of games that end unified in the Garden scenarios (PRD §22) against today's; the 0.044 kin share (K = 4) is the dial, and the tolerance can be raised.
  - *Unification starved*: the distance to the fungal point is larger in more dimensions (the host cap in S2); measured by time-to-unity.
  - *Cliff*: a foe memory that makes a border war permanent in a minute (S4's gate).
  - *Invisible*: four odour axes that the player cannot see; the lens and the Learn entry are in the plan, not an afterthought.
  - *Cost creep*: the sparse bands growing with continuous host odours; the bench catches it.
- **Owner decisions needed:** §Open questions.
